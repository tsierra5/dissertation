#Introduction

[First sentence.]

My grandmother's first language was Cheyenne. This she held in common with her parents, who lived and raised her in the Cheyenne community of Hammon, Oklahoma. All of them spoke English as well, and my grandmother sat through many formal English lessons at Chilocco Indian School. After boarding school and serving in the navy for some years, when my grandmother had children, she raised them to speak English rather than Cheyenne. It was a language reserved mostly for conversation among her generation and those before. Following along this trajectory, my cousins and I were not passed on any Cheyenne language knowledge beyond sparing words and phrases that still held out use among family members.

Though they were present, the full effects of the language divide in my family were unknown to me growing up. At the time, it generated some curious situations and little mysteries when relating to my grandmother and my great grandparents. There was also a persistent and keen sense of wishing for my grandmother to teach me Cheyenne, but that for an endless series of reasons, was never fulfilled. The import of these mysteries, and the sense of not having something that I needed as a result of this language divide, is the impetus for this dissertation.
