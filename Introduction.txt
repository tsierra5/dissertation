#Preface

[First sentence.]

My grandmother's first language was Cheyenne. This she held in common with her parents, who lived and raised her in the Cheyenne community of Hammon, Oklahoma. All of them spoke English as well, and my grandmother sat through many formal English lessons at Chilocco Indian School. After boarding school and serving in the navy for some years, when my grandmother had children, she raised them to speak English rather than Cheyenne. It was a language reserved mostly for conversation among her generation and those before. Following along this trajectory, my cousins and I were not passed on any Cheyenne language knowledge beyond sparing words and phrases that still held out use among family members.

Though they were present, the full effects of the language divide in my family were unknown to me growing up. At the time, it generated some curious situations and little mysteries when relating to my grandmother and my great grandparents. There was also a persistent and keen sense of wishing for my grandmother to teach me Cheyenne, but that for an endless series of reasons, was never fulfilled. The import of these mysteries, and the sense of not having something that I needed as a result of this language divide, is the impetus for this dissertation.

As I've recounted these stories so far, the sense of language learning that is involved here stops quite short of the most immediate sense. Most people, when asked what it is involved in language learning, will say more than reading dictionary entries and learning vocabulary. Regular practice and speech is required as well. However, in situations of language disconnection and endangerment, even the first, most passive, steps of language learning can pay off impressively-- and this is what I hope to have demonstrated with the previous stories. It is my conviction that language revitalization is something that should be made as accessible as possible to all those for whom it presents a therapeutic benefit. One way of making it accessible is to show that there are varying levels of engagement and commitment that can be involved in revitalization, and to motivate any and all effort, no matter how introductory. Even cultivating receptive skills alone is worth pursuing, as I hope to have illustrated.

Even if the same kind of seamless reflex that first-language speaker has is not achievable for a second-language speaker, or even if it would take many years of reinforcement to get close, I contend that there is value in the practicing anyway. The value in it, against Nowak's line of justification (c.f. Chapter One), does not have anything to do with the first-language speech of our ancestors or whether that speech is interpretable by their descendants in order to grasp the social identity being formed by those ancestors. After all, the ancestors are not around to hear us and speak with us, at least not in any usual sense. But without the involvement of even our memories of first-language speakers, how should we conceptualize the value in my family continuing to speak Cheyenne when it is just us second-language speakers left to do it? It is precisely this situation which still has gotten no treatment unto itself in all of my discussion of revitalization up to this point.
