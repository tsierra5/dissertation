Nowak (2019) argues for an autonomy-based view of the value of language. Specifically, the view is that language is a necessary condition for autonomous actions in the social domain, because it plays an enabling role on one's ability to act autonomously in constructing her social identity. What makes his view especially relevant to us here is that Nowak intends for his account to “justify claims about the preservation, protection, or accommodation of minority languages” (1). Naturally, language revitalizationists will be interested to hear more. 

Nowak's view rests on two major claims. The first is that every language makes available some potential speech acts which are not available in any other language. The second claim is that, by affording agents with a distinct background menu of speech acts to choose from, languages function like contexts of choice,2 without which an agent's speech cannot be rendered socially meaningful. And, meaningful choices about how to speak are a large part of what constitutes an agent's social identity. 

Most would likely agree that our choices and actions must first have meaning assigned to them if we are going to be said to enjoy autonomy with respect to those same actions and choices. So, if the speech act potential of a language forms a context of choice necessary for crafting a meaningful social identity, then we should conclude that language is necessary to an agent's enjoyment of autonomy when constructing her social identity. 

At this point, however, Nowak’s argument only shows that access to some language or other is necessary for an agent's communicative choices to count as autonomous shapings of her social identity. If, however, the argument is going to justify claims about the preservation, protection, or accommodation of minority languages, then it needs to show why an agent might require access to some language in particular and not another. Nowak argues speakers require continued access to their first language(s), since only then does a speaker have the kind of full-fledged language mastery that affords maximal control over how to speak and thus how to come across to others.
