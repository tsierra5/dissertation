Next consider another case involving two generations, each speaking a different L1. Let us suppose this time that the elders in question are 1) actually elderly and 2) bilinguals who are the last remaining speakers of their first language L1x and who also speak the younger generation’s language, L1y. Meanwhile, the young people start off as monolingual, only speaking L1y. This situation represents very late stages of language endangerment, such as we currently see with many Native American languages as a result of targeted linguicide and forced assimilation policies in place for several generations. 

Now further suppose that the young people undertake to learn L1x from their elders, and they do manage to become bilingual in L1y and L1x. But, some time after this successful transfer of the language takes place, the elders eventually pass away, leaving behind the younger generation that speaks L1x only as a second language. Again, an account that locates the favored sense of autonomy within first languages thus has no way of explaining per se why the young people should continue to speak L1x. And in this case, whatever additional considerations might have been in play about intergenerational intelligibility are rendered moot once the first language speakers have passed.
