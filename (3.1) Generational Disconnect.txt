Consider a scenario involving two distinct generations in a community. Call the first language of the older generation L1x and the first language of the younger generation L1y. Suppose that both generations speak some L2 as a common second language in which they communicate, but only the elders speak L1x and only the young people speak L1y. Further suppose that the elders will be the last ones to speak L1x if the language is not transmitted to the younger generation. But for whatever reason, none of the younger generation is learning L1x.  

In this situation, each generation is speaking their first language (L1_), and they have a mutual language (L2) for communication purposes. By Nowak’s lights, then, all is well. The elders have their autonomy via first language mastery and the younger generation has theirs. In terms of autonomy, the situation does not appear to be defective for either party. 

On the other hand, both parties do sacrifice some autonomy when speaking L2. It is their second language, after all. Even so, this does not per se amount to any reason why anyone should learn the other’s first language. But the revitalizationist attitude is that ideally the younger generation should be learning L1x, because otherwise L1x will eventually fall out of use. Such a worry characterizes early stages of language endangerment. Yet there does not appear to be any autonomy-based reason to address it, because the important condition for autonomy is met: each generation enjoys first language mastery among itself, while still being able to communicate with members of the other. There is no built-in regard for the status of a vulnerable language. But this is precisely what is needed to motivate special efforts to ensure its future.
