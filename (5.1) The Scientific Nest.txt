The first nest, what I call the scientific nest, is the one that houses the institutional engine of academic science. It is built for producing knowledge according to standards and procedures that are meant to preserve objectivity. 

The scientific nest is the one that linguists operate out of, collectively gathering and analyzing data from as many languages as possible in order to get insight into the range of linguistic properties and structures. 

When asked why we should revitalize languages, one situated in the scientific nest will take an "omniscient" or third-person view by attending to the worldwide situation of language endangerment and consider the impacts of waning linguistic diversity. The decline of linguistic diversity might be considered bad in itself, analogous to how some environmental preservationists view the loss of biodiversity. Instrumental reasons may also be cited, such as preventing the loss of sources for data, or perhaps the loss of cognitive diversity, or the erosion of cultures, or the disappearance of folk knowledge systems. But all of these answers from the scientific nest are marked by conceptualizing language endangerment at the level of a global phenomenon, and therefore seeing revitalization as a collective, global intervention.
