#Names cases
	Apart from the words and phrases that remained intact in family usage, there is also a part of the Cheyenne language that survived for us and which enjoyed a more special regard and attention than so much else. So long as my grandmother lived, she ensured that each new family member was given, in addition to their English name, a name in Cheyenne as well. It is the job of the parents, as well as the named child herself, to safeguard her name by reciting and practicing it from time to time, making sure not to forget, and gradually honing her pronunciation. It was a semi-frequent occurence when I was growing up for my father or my grandmother to summon me and ask me if I knew my name in Cheyenne.
	I cannot remember who first told me my name or when it was told to me. But I do not remember ever answering the question of my name incorrectly. Something in me must have sensed from early on that it was very important information, in a category unto itself and something to be treated very specially.
	In North American Indigenous communities, this is a relatively common pattern-- non-speaking descendants and their children are given Indian names*[fn: no scare quotes] from the elders in their families or others in their communities who still carry the Indigenous language. The rationale and significance behind this practice might vary profoundly from tribe to tribe, and even across families, but there is no mistaking that Indian names, for so many of us, serve as a firm last stop of language retention.
	There are intuitive reasons why names should be a remaining foothold for language use even as they are experiencing decline. Names serve as linguistic bedrock for identity; they can, through more or less convoluted paths, provide information about an individual's ethnic identity and cultural affiliation perhaps earlier on than any other things a person might communicate through speech.
	In addition to performing foundational social functions, names have also long been observed to arrive at their reference in a unique way. Consider the classic remarks of philosopher John Stuart Mill, in his attending to the fact that names do not inherently communicate any qualities about their named objects:

	"A non-connotative term is one which signifies a subject only, or an attribute only. A connotative term is one which denotes a subject and implies an attribute. [...] Proper names are not connotative; they denote the individuals who are called by them, but they do not indicate or imply any attributes as belonging to those individuals. When we name a child by the name Paul or a dog by the name Caesar, these names are simply marks used to enable those individuals to be made subjects of discourse." (Mill, Section 5, Book I, Chapter i)

	Names appear to refer to the individuals which they name in a rather direct way, without necessarily calling up or offering any information about what the individual is like. In short, names do not describe their objects, as other parts of language do. This likely contributes to the sense in which they are a bedrock part of an individual's identity.
	It has been observed, most notably by philosopher Saul Kripke, that the reference of a name is fixed in a way that has largely to do with the individual's local social and conversational history. Consider the following passage, amended slightly in the spirit of fun:

	"Someone, let’s say, a baby, is born; his parents call him by a certain name. They talk about him to their friends. Other people meet him. Though various sorts of talk the name is spread from link to link as if by a chain. A speaker who is on the far end of this chain, who has heard about, say [Roman Nose], in the market place or elsewhere, may be referring to [Henry Roman Nose] even though he can’t remember from whom he first heard of [Roman Nose] or from whom he ever heard of [Roman Nose]. He knows that [Roman Nose] was a famous [Cheyenne chief]. A certain passage of communication reaching ultimately to the man himself does reach the speaker. He then is referring to [Roman Nose] even though he can’t identify him uniquely." (Kripke 91)


In the Cheyenne way, a baby gets a name from a relative, often a grandparent. That relative will either make up a new name, or they can pick a family member's name to give to the baby. In the latter case, if the given name comes from a living family member, that person may change their name, presumably since their old one now belongs to the baby.  My Grandma Marcy gave her name to me when I was born, and that name was made up for her by a relative (I believe her grandfather or great grandfather). Then after she passed her name to me, she called herself by another name.

Our Cheyenne names function like family heirlooms; by recycling names, we hold them in the space of generational memory and keep custody of them. In this way our names link us to our ancestors, much like how wearing a family ring or wedding dress creates a connection to the ancestor it belonged to. In this way we manage to maintain relationships with our kin even after they are not living. 

What I hope to have shown with these stories are some roles that language plays in establishing important relationships between kin, or between a descendant and her ancestors. For in understanding our ancestors’ words as they understood them, and in bearing their names as they bore them, we maintain bonds with our ancestors that deepen the recognition that we are of a kind. The ability to inhabit such relationships to one's kin gives community members reason to learn and to pass down their language.
