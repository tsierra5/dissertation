	We have walked, now, through the three hypothetical cases of language endangerment that each pose a different hurdle for Nowak's argument for language preservation on the basis of protecting individual autonomy. The original argument is meant to analyze and defend the interest that first-language speakers have in continued access to their first-language communities. That interest is explicated in terms of the unique speech act space that a language affords its speakers, which in turn makes possible certain ways of manifesting the speaker's social identity that are not possible in another language. Given that languages cannot be exchanged for one another in this respect, and given that speakers are fluent in their first languages to a degree that they are not in their second languages, individuals face an irreplaceable kind of loss as to the control they are able to exert over how they occupy social space when they lose access to their first languages.
	In the generational silo case, we saw that in situations where language decline has progressed to the point where generations of a community speak different first languages, the reasoning which flows from protecting first languages will not directly be able to motivate reparative language learning. Since language learners in this situation will necessarily be second-language speakers, they cannot employ the same degree of linguistic skill that their first-language interlocutors do, and cannot offer the same degree of fineness in registering their interlocutors' social identities as they come through uniquely in that language. Hence, a fair amount of language learners will not be able to offer the conversational fluency needed to redeem the good that is conceptualized in Nowak's original argument. If the good that Nowak argues we should protect cannot be achieved, it seems unclear what the aim of language learning would be. At best, it seems that the good of first-language enabled autonomy can stand in to motivate ameliorative efforts to make up for as much of this good as possible, even if it cannot be restored entirely.

	The resurrection case is the most extreme of revitalization scenarios. In the abstract, it might seem rather out of touch to even consider this kind of case. In chapter two, I will offer some considerations, however, that aim to concretize this scenario as one where compelling reasons for "resurrection" exist in certain contexts.
	At the very least, we will need to reach for some other tools than purely the ones we have tried to make use of so far in order to motivate, and perhaps even to justify, a devoted revitalization of extinct languages, as these seem to be a special case that is not properly touched by Nowak's argument for language preservation. On one hand this is entirely understandable, because he is primarily interested to center language protections on the interests of living individuals, insofar as the argument hinges on a basic shared understanding that we should not deprive people of autonomy, all things considered. However, I think it is entirely possible to leverage the interests of living individuals to argue for the good of revitalizing extinct languages, and by extension those which are not extinct either. I will develop my own account in attempt to do just that in the next chapter.
