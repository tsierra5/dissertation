Now consider a language, call it Lx, that is completely extinct; no one speaks or understands this language anymore. However, imagine that Lx happens to be in the lucky position of being extremely well-documented– so much so that anyone could learn the language if she dedicated herself to studying it, although it would be a lonely endeavor. 

Many will agree that it would be a shame not to see such a language reclaimed. But since anyone reclaiming Lx is necessarily a second-language learner, according to Nowak’s view Lx might as well be left alone, as it could never afford someone the same level of autonomy as their first language. And in this case, additional concerns about intergenerational intelligibility never even get off the ground, as there is no living language community associated with Lx to begin with.


	In contrast with the previous two cases, the fact that there are no living speakers of L whatsoever means that no sort of inertia from the interests of existing speakers can move us in the right direction this time. We can grant that the level of preservation that we start out with in this case, i.e. that of the language being thoroughly documented, could be motivated as part of an amelioration during the time in which there did exist first language speakers of L, or by our amended version of Nowak's argument in the second case, when there were living speakers of any adequate level of fluency. For these efforts toward documentation could have been motivated in the hopes that future generations would use these resources to facilitate the continued generational transfer of L. But if, despite these efforts, there is no uptake from any potential language learners, and we find ourselves in a world where L as a language now exists only as it is reflected in documentation, then the previous grounds for working to preserve the language seem to have run their course and there is simply nothing left to do.
	While it might be a manner of contention whether the situation is or should be one of finality, I think that there might be some strands of a revitalizationist attitude according to which it would be good and worthwhile to effectively “resurrect” language L. Now, thus far I have argued that Novak's view can be made to justify  the protection of certain languages with a bent towards what would be lost for their speakers if they become cut off from their languages. Given a situation where that ship has sailed as fully as it can, I think that any argument that will justify or motivate the revitalization of a language completely anew for any of its learners will have to do almost entirely with what those potential learners stand to gain from their language learning.
	Here, again, Novak's way of developing a sense in which languages, every one of them, provides and enables a unique speech act space can be made to do some lifting. (Because you might be able to run a kind of bizarre argument on which people might have an interest in instantiating the unique speech-active potential of some particular language.)
